The short version · 1 minute

“Sign in with Google” tells an app who you are.It never sees your password.

Google checks your password, then gives the app a short-lived key that opens only what you agreed to share. The app collects that key straight from Google, out of sight.

What this means for you

Signing in with Google is safer than making yet another password, as long as you read what the app asks to see. You can switch off any app's access at any time in the security settings of your Google Account.

Words you'll meet

A short-lived digital key. Whoever holds it can open what it unlocks, so it must stay secret.

Open Authorization (OAuth)
The open standard behind "Sign in with…" buttons. Version 2.0 is the one in use.

2.1

Why not give the app your password?

Because then the app could do anything you can do, for as long as it likes. And every app would be one more place your password could leak from.

With Google sign-in, the app gets a narrow, short-lived key instead.

2.3

What exactly is the key the app gets?

It's an access token: a long random string that Google recognises. It works like a hotel key card.

A hotel key card opens your room, not every room, and stops working when you check out. Losing it is annoying, not a disaster.
Quick checkYou find an access token in a log file from last week. How worried should you be?Show answer

Not very: it stopped working after about an hour. Apps can also hold longer-lived "refresh" tokens, and those must be stored safely. The deep dive explains them.

2.4

Why is the key handed over out of sight?

Anything that passes through your browser can end up in its history, in add-ons or on a shared computer. So the valuable part is done directly between the app and Google. Scroll through it.

Google sends you back to the app with a one-time code. This trip goes through your browser, so the code could be seen. That's fine: on its own, it's useless.
The app calls Google directly, server to server, sending the code plus its own secret. Your browser is not involved.
Google checks both and answers with the access token, on the same private line.
The key never touched your browser. Even if someone copied the code, they couldn't use it without the app's secret.

Behind the screen

  1. You click Sign in with Google in the app.
  2. The app sends your browser to Google, with a note saying who is asking and what it wants.
  3. You type your password and click Allow. Only Google sees the password.
  4. Google sends your browser back to the app with a one-time code.
  5. Out of sight, the app sends the code and its own secret straight to Google.
  6. Google checks both and returns an access token: the key.
  7. The app shows the key to Google and gets back your email address.
  8. You're signed in. The app never saw your password.

What about the edge cases?

You remove the app from your Google AccountIts tokens stop working. Next time, it has to ask you again.
A fake page looks like GoogleCheck the address bar says accounts.google.com before typing your password. The registered return address stops codes going to a fake app, but it can't stop you typing a password into a fake page.
You sign out of the appThe app forgets you, but you stay signed in to Google. Signing out of Google is a separate step.
The app asks again laterNormal if its key expired and it has no refresh token, or if it now wants more than before. Read the list again.
