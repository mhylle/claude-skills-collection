# Why the rules are what they are

The evidence behind the skill's rules, for when someone asks "why". "Strong" means
many controlled experiments agree; "limited" means sensible practice with little
hard testing. Effect sizes (d or g) measure how big a difference is against how
much people normally vary: about 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large.

| Rule | What research found | Source | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answer first, three layers | 79% of test users scanned new pages; 16% read word by word | Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web" (1997); Minto, *The Pyramid Principle* | moderate |
| A diagram per idea | Words plus pictures beat words alone (median d = 1.67, 5 comparisons) | Mayer (2017), *J. Computer Assisted Learning* | strong |
| No decoration | Cutting extra material: d = 0.70. Interesting but off-topic extras *lower* learning (g = −0.33), more so when visual | Mayer (2017); Rey (2012); Sundararajan & Adesope (2020) | strong |
| "What to notice", highlights, one orange thing | Cues that show what matters: d = 0.46 | Mayer (2017) | strong |
| "Words you'll meet", abbreviations spelled out | Knowing the key terms first: d = 0.46. Plain-language guidance: define abbreviations at first use, and use few | Mayer (2017); US Federal Plain Language Guidelines | strong |
| Up to five sections, step controls | Lessons in reader-paced segments: d = 0.70, strongest for newcomers | Mayer (2017) | strong |
| Quick checks after each section | Answering from memory beats reading again, across many settings | testing-effect research, e.g. Roediger & Karpicke | strong |
| Guess first (full mode) | Attempting an answer before learning it improves retention, even when the guess is wrong | pretesting research, e.g. Pan & Sana (2021) | strong |
| Headings as questions | Curiosity comes from a small gap someone feels they can close | Loewenstein (1994) | moderate |
| Bars, not pies, for amounts | Position and length are read accurately; angle, area and colour shade much less so | Cleveland & McGill (1984); a 2026 follow-up shows the order shifts with the task | strong |
| Comparison, then the real mechanism | Moving from concrete to abstract helps transfer, especially with consistent colours | McNeil & Fyfe (2012) | moderate |
| Animations play by themselves | Animations beat static pictures when they show change (medium effect); animations that play by themselves did as well as or better than learner-paced ones | Höffler & Leutner (2007); Berney & Bétrancourt (2016) | moderate |
| Interactions must make you think | Interactive features that don't require thinking add effort without improving learning | Berney & Bétrancourt (2016); Fiorella & Mayer (2015) | moderate |
| Read aloud (full mode) | Spoken words beside pictures beat printed words beside pictures (about d = 0.7), weaker for experts | Mayer (2017) | strong |
| Pacing by reading, a Pace control | Speed changes *what* people notice: fast shows the big picture, slow the details; learners given control choose slow; older viewers struggle with fast animations | Meyer, Rasch & Schnotz (2010); De Koning et al. (2011) | moderate |
| Exact durations (beat 0.65 s, reveal about 1 s) | Set from reading speed (about 4 words a second) plus time to look; not tested as such | design judgement | limited |
| Progressive disclosure (pages, deep dive) | Widely used; little controlled testing | Nielsen Norman Group | limited |
